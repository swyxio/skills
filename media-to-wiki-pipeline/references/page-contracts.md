# Portable page contracts and quality

Choose the page's reader purpose before drafting. Page type is independent of source format: a podcast can support a reader, a biography, an organization profile and a topic synthesis, but those outputs should not repeat the same summary.

## Source reader: talk, episode, interview, article or paper

Explain this source's specific contribution so the page stands alone and helps readers investigate the original. Preserve its substantive mechanisms, examples, conditions, limitations and disagreements. A recorded read-along preserves useful progression and personality; a paper explainer may follow problem/method/results/limitations. Choose structure for source genre, not a universal outline.

Distinguish what the source establishes from later clarification or related work. An interview's host and guest claims remain distinguishable. Preserve memorable source language selectively and faithfully; avoid turning a lively exchange into a report about reporting. Link original sections, timestamps or figures where available; unavailable locators are not invented. Follow video-talk-to-essay for recording-specific craft and visuals.

## Person profile

Explain who the person is, their distinctive contributions, relevant development and recurring ideas across their work. A speaker is a person, not merely a conference participant. Use person-profile-writing for identity, chronology and attribution. A new source can change a supported claim or add an important contribution; it does not justify rewriting an accepted biography into its latest appearance.

Select concrete contributions and explain why they matter without inventing impact, motives or causal history. Distinguish participation, collaboration, authorship and ownership. Roles have dates and sources; event credits do not establish present employment. Evidence-poor profiles can be short without becoming generic descriptions of a profession.

## Organization profile

Explain the entity's purpose, substantive work, whom it serves and what distinguishes it. Adapt to the entity:

- Companies: products/services, users, useful history, technical contributions and relevant business context.
- Labs or universities: research areas, contributions, institutional relationships and relevant history.
- Nonprofits or communities: mission, actual programs or activities, participants served and governance when pertinent.
- Projects or collectives: scope, maintained work, authorship/governance and supported institutional relationships.

These are selection options, not mandatory sections. A product, lab, parent company and community are not interchangeable entities. A person's experiment does not become an employer's commercial product merely through affiliation.

Lead with the organization itself, not archive coverage or verification. Develop supported history or enduring contributions instead of listing announcements, executives, papers or vendors. Use scale figures only when their definition and date help explain the subject; distinguish adoption, funding, valuation, revenue and run rate. Do not force financial metrics onto noncommercial entities or pad sparse evidence. Important known closure, acquisition or operating transitions must not be obscured by older true facts.

A single credible primary source can support a modest profile. Missing optional founders, current executives, finance or research details does not require a dossier. Repetition, empty praise or a talk walkthrough can be editorial defects even when their individual facts are true.

## Topic page

Help a reader understand a bounded subject and navigate its most useful sources. Define scope, aliases and exclusions; distinguish genuinely different concepts rather than merging labels by keyword overlap. Explain mechanisms, use cases, tradeoffs and important distinctions with concrete sourced examples.

Synthesize complementary sources and conflicting positions without fabricating agreement, causal relationships or completeness. Distinguish chronology/version differences from genuine disagreements. A corpus is not automatically representative of a field, and source frequency is not evidence of consensus or importance.

Offer an annotated reading/listening path: explain what each selected source adds, for whom it is useful and, where warranted, what to read first. Prefer useful coverage to an exhaustive list. A keyword introduction followed by cards is not a substantive topic synthesis. A sparse corpus may support a brief source guide rather than a comprehensive explainer; label that scope honestly.

When evidence lacks an important perspective, identify the consequential limitation concisely or collect additional sources within scope. Do not treat topical silence as proof that a technique, view or organization does not exist. Keep topic claims and source associations traceable so a subset refresh can replace only affected explanations or recommendations.

## Quality without site-specific examples

Evaluate factual support and editorial usefulness separately. An accepted page should:

- Answer a distinct reader question early and identify its particular subject.
- Explain substantive contributions or mechanisms through concrete supported details, rather than name lists or generic category prose.
- Develop a coherent account with useful navigation; omit duplication and material that belongs on another page type.
- Preserve ownership, attribution, time/version scope and consequential uncertainty without narrating the audit process.
- Use meaningful verified links and visuals when they add understanding; source locators and figure counts are not quality quotas.
- Remain useful when read alone and direct readers to the appropriate original or related work.

Report factual errors separately from editorial defects. Formatting preferences, different valid emphasis or missing optional details are suggestions, not automatic blockers. Repair the affected unit and preserve strong accepted text. A valid JSON result, checked citations or a passing screenshot does not alone establish good writing.

Complete approved pages from AIE or another corpus can illustrate depth, selection and reader experience. Extract the properties that make them good; do not copy their voice, route structure, length, facts or recurring headings. The rubric works without those examples. For transfer validation, use a small unfamiliar corpus spanning requested page types, sparse/rich evidence and ambiguous cases; evaluate first without site-specific examples. Include a resume/subset-refresh exercise where applicable. This validates portability, not search ranking or full-corpus coverage.

## Search-facing presentation

Give each page substantive value and a distinct intent: understanding a source, a person, an organization or a subject. Use accurate descriptive titles, summaries, canonical identity and useful links between those pages. Avoid keyword padding, near-duplicate entity introductions and creating pages solely for every detected term. Canonical URLs, structured data and indexability follow the destination's existing implementation; do not transplant another site's conventions or promise ranking improvements without measurement.
